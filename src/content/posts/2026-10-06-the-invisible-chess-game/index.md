*Part 1 of a 4-part series on politics, visibility, performance and the new career flex.*

I meet many leaders in my work at the Microsoft Innovation Hub and in the Office of the CTO. When a big transformation programme stalls, the cause is rarely the technology. Most of the time it is a quiet fight about scope, ownership or who gets to present to the board.

Nobody calls it politics. But it is.

That is why I spent a weekend with three long podcast conversations between Ryan Peterman and **Ethan Evans**, a retired Amazon Vice President. Evans spent more than 15 years at Amazon, led organisations of over 800 people, and worked on products such as Prime Video, the Amazon Appstore and Prime Gaming ([ethanevans.com/about](https://www.ethanevans.com/about)). Today he writes the *Level Up* newsletter.

The conversations are honest, sometimes uncomfortably so. Here is what I took from them, checked against public sources, and then pushed back on.

## Politics is not a bug, it is a system property

Evans' starting point is simple: politics happens wherever people compete for limited things like scope, headcount, promotions and attention. He says it got *more* common as Amazon's growth slowed. When a company grows fast, it is easier to get new people than to take scope from a peer. When growth stops, people start fighting over what already exists.

That matches what I see with customers. In growth phases, AI pilots multiply. In cost-cutting phases, the same pilots turn into turf.

**Label:** Evans' account, consistent with my own observations.

## Reorgs carry hidden passengers

Evans describes reorgs as moments when many agendas get loaded onto the same ship before it leaves the dock. There is the official business reason. Then there are the unofficial ones: give a peer enough scope to get promoted, move a difficult person, or quietly shrink a team.

He tells of people who came to him saying their boss had put them under a peer *so that the peer's team would look bigger*.

None of this is in an org chart. It is still real.

**Label:** Evans' account. I could not verify specific reorg decisions independently, and you should not assume every reorg works this way.

## Headcount as status, despite the leadership principles

The most revealing part was about **empire building**. Evans defines it as focusing on the raw number of people rather than on impact, and he calls it a bad thing.

Then he explains why it happens anyway. Amazon's Frugality principle says, in Amazon's own words: *"There are no extra points for growing headcount, budget size, or fixed expense"* ([Amazon Leadership Principles](https://www.aboutamazon.eu/who-we-are/leadership-principles)). But, according to Evans, when a promotion level gets tied to an organisation size (he remembers 75 to 80 being intended and 90 being written down in some orgs), the incentive wins over the principle.

This is the most important lesson in the series for me: **people follow what you measure, not what you write on the wall.** Every AI programme I review has a "values" slide. The interesting question is always what gets measured and rewarded.

**Label:** The principle is documented policy. The promotion thresholds are Evans' memory and may have varied by org and year.

## How I read this as a leader

I don't think the answer is to become cynical. The answer is to make politics *legible*:

- **Name the incentives.** If headcount or budget unlocks promotion, say so openly, and then decide if that is what you want.
- **Separate scope from status.** Give ownership because of outcomes, not to fix someone's promo case.
- **Explain reorgs honestly.** People can usually take a hard reason. What they can't take is finding out the real reason later.

## Challenging the advice

**Simon Sinek** would probably say Evans describes a *finite game*: fixed players, fixed rules, clear winners. In *The Infinite Game*, Sinek argues that business is an infinite game, where the goal is to keep playing and to advance a "just cause", not to beat the colleague next to you ([simonsinek.com](https://simonsinek.com/books/the-infinite-game/)). In *Leaders Eat Last*, he describes a "Circle of Safety". When leaders make people feel safe inside the organisation, energy goes outward to the customer instead of inward to defence.

Seen through that lens, empire building is not mainly a character flaw. It is a sign that the circle of safety is too small. People hoard headcount because headcount feels like protection.

I think both are true. Evans gives you a map of the board as it is. Sinek reminds leaders that they choose which game the board rewards.

## My takeaways

1. Politics grows when growth slows. Expect it, and plan for it in transformation programmes.
2. Incentives beat principles. Audit what you measure.
3. You can understand the chess game without becoming a cynical player.
