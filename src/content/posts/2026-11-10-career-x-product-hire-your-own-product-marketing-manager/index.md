Engineers often believe that great products sell themselves. Product marketers know better. The graveyard of technology is full of excellent products nobody heard of.

Careers have the same graveyard. It is full of brilliant people who believed that good work speaks for itself. It does not. Good work whispers. Somebody has to amplify it.

In [episode four](?post=career-x-product-pricing-your-promotion) we priced the product. This episode is about distribution: how your impact travels to the people who decide, when you are not in the room. You need a product marketing manager. Until you can afford one, it is you.

## Brand is what people say when you leave the room

Your brand is not your LinkedIn headline. It is the sentence other people use to describe you to someone who has never met you.

- "She is the one who untangles impossible customer escalations."
- "He is the person who turns architecture into something executives understand."
- "Ask them - they built the community everyone uses."

If people cannot complete that sentence, you do not have a brand. You have a reputation for being nice. That is valuable, but it is not distribution.

## Positioning: be known for something

Product marketing starts with positioning: for whom, for what problem, and why you instead of the alternative. Apply it to yourself.

> For **[customer]**, who struggles with **[problem]**, I am the **[category]** who **[distinct value]**, unlike **[alternative]**.

Mine, roughly: for enterprise leaders who struggle to turn AI ambition into results, I am the architect who connects strategy, systems, and people - unlike pure strategists who never built the thing, or pure engineers who never sat in the boardroom. "From the server room to the boardroom" is not a tagline for decoration. It is positioning.

Narrow positioning feels risky. It is the opposite. Being known for one thing makes you memorable enough to be thought of for many.

## Visibility without self-promotion

Most people hate the idea of self-promotion, and they are right to. Loud people who narrate their own greatness erode trust. But visibility and self-promotion are different things.

**Self-promotion** says: look at me.
**Visibility** says: here is something useful.

The most effective visibility strategies I have seen are generous by design:

1. **Narrate the impact, not the activity.** "We cut onboarding from three weeks to four days" beats "I worked really hard on onboarding."
2. **Ship artefacts.** A reusable template, a decision framework, a reference architecture. Artefacts travel further than meetings.
3. **Learn in public.** Share what you are figuring out, not just what you have figured out. This blog exists for that reason.
4. **Credit others loudly.** Public recognition of your team's work signals leadership and builds advocates.
5. **Teach.** Running bootcamps for more than 1,200 architects did more for my visibility than any status update ever did - because it created value for every person in the room.

## The distribution channels

| Channel | Reach | What works |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your manager | Highest leverage | Regular short updates, a one-sentence impact statement they can repeat |
| Skip-level and leadership | Decision makers | Occasional crisp briefings on outcomes, not activity |
| Peers and cross-team partners | Reputation | Reliability, generosity, shared wins |
| Internal communities | Scale | Content, sessions, office hours, reusable assets |
| External presence | Market value | Writing, speaking, open contributions |

You do not need every channel. You need the right two or three for your customer.

## Awards as distribution

Here is an honest observation about awards: they are a distribution event as much as a recognition event. An award takes impact that a small group knew about and broadcasts it to an entire organisation.

When I received the Pinnacle Award, the most valuable outcome was not the trophy. It was that the work - the communities, the people enabled, the customer outcomes - became visible to people who would never otherwise have seen it. Conversations opened that had been closed.

But note the order. The impact came first. The nomination came from people who had seen the impact. Somebody had to write the story. Which brings us to the most underrated skill in this whole series.

## Write the story others will retell

Product marketers write the narrative so sales teams can repeat it. Do the same for your advocates.

- One sentence that captures who you are.
- One story that proves it: situation, action, outcome.
- One number that makes it memorable.

When someone asks your manager or sponsor "what is so special about them?", they should be able to answer in thirty seconds without notes.

## The inner game of visibility

Michael Gervais talks about FOPO - the fear of other people's opinions - as one of the biggest constraints on performance. It is also the biggest constraint on visibility. People do not share their work because they fear looking arrogant, wrong, or unfinished.

The antidote is not confidence. It is purpose. If you share something because it is useful to someone else, the fear has less to hold on to. Write for the person who needs it, not for the critics who might judge it.

## The unspoken

**Invisible excellence is often rewarded as average.** Not because leaders are careless, but because they cannot reward what they never saw.

**Your brand is being built whether you manage it or not.** If you do not shape it, it is shaped by your last escalation, your loudest meeting, or your absence.

**Noise is not brand.** Constant status posts and meeting monologues create visibility, but the wrong kind. Distribution without substance accelerates the moment you get found out.

## Low, good, exceptional

| | Low | Good | Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brand | Undefined or negative | Known as reliable | Known for something specific and valuable |
| Visibility | Invisible or noisy | Visible to their team | Visible through value others share |
| Story | Activity lists | Achievements | A narrative others can retell |
| Generosity | Takes credit | Shares credit | Builds others' visibility |

## Try this week

1. **Ask three people** to describe you in one sentence to someone who does not know you. Compare the answers to the brand you want.
2. **Write your positioning statement** using the template above.
3. **Ship one artefact** - a template, a short write-up, a lesson learned - that is useful beyond your team.

> Good work whispers. Generous work echoes.
