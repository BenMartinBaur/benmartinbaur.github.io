Early in my career I built something I was proud of. It was technically elegant, well documented, and nobody used it. I had solved a problem I found interesting. I had not solved a problem anyone was paying for.

It took me a while to understand that this is the most common failure mode in corporate careers. Not low effort. Not low skill. **Building for a customer who does not exist.**

In [episode one](?post=career-x-product-you-are-the-product) we framed your career as a product. This episode is about the first block of the Career Product Canvas: the customer. Who is paying for your product - and do you actually know what they need?

## Your salary is a subscription

Your employer pays you every month. That is a recurring subscription. Like every subscription, it is renewed - formally or informally - based on perceived value. And like every subscription business, the real money is not in retention. It is in expansion: a bigger scope, a higher level, a premium tier.

That reframing changes the question from "What is my job?" to "Why does my customer keep renewing - and what would make them upgrade?"

## The three customers

Every corporate career has three customers. Most people serve one well and ignore the other two.

**1. The business.** The company's strategy, revenue, cost, risk, and customers. The business does not know your name, but it decides which problems get funded. If your work is not connected to a funded priority, it is a hobby with a badge.

**2. Your manager.** The person who represents you in every room you are not in. Your manager is not just your boss. They are your most important distribution channel and, often, your first sponsor. Their success and yours are coupled.

**3. The community.** Your peers, your team, the people you mentor, the wider practice. The community decides your reputation. It is slower to form and harder to fake, and it compounds.

| Customer | What they buy | What happens if you ignore them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The business | Outcomes on funded priorities | Your work is "nice" but not rewarded |
| Your manager | Fewer problems, more leverage, no surprises | You become a cost, not an asset |
| The community | Generosity, expertise, reliability | Nobody advocates for you |

The exceptional performers I have worked with serve all three, and they understand that the three are connected. Solve a business problem in a way that makes your manager look good and lifts your community, and you have created a product that sells itself.

## Discover the real problem

Product teams talk about jobs to be done: the underlying progress a customer is trying to make. A customer does not want a drill. They want a hole. Actually, they want a shelf on the wall.

Your manager also has jobs to be done. They are rarely written in your goals.

- Reduce the number of escalations landing on their desk.
- Have a credible story for their own leadership.
- Ship the commitment they made two levels up.
- Grow people so they can scale themselves.
- Not be surprised.

When I led a team of 36 across EMEA, the people I relied on most were not always the most technically brilliant. They were the ones who understood which of those jobs mattered most to me that quarter - and quietly took one off my plate.

> The fastest way to manage up is to make your manager's job easier without being asked.

## Managing up is product management

"Managing up" sounds political. It is not. It is customer success.

Here is the framework I use and teach:

1. **Discover.** Ask what success looks like for your manager this half. Ask what keeps them up at night. Listen more than you pitch.
2. **Align.** Translate their priorities into two or three outcomes you will own. Write them down. Confirm them.
3. **Deliver.** Ship against those outcomes. Show progress early, not just at the end.
4. **Report.** Give a short, regular update: what moved, what is blocked, what you need. Bad news early, never late.
5. **Renew.** Every few months, revisit. Priorities shift. Products that do not adapt get churned.

This is not sycophancy. It is respect for your customer's time. Ethan Evans, a former Amazon VP, makes a similar point in his conversations about [how corporate politics work](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6WaeGfLnRvc): understand what the people around you are trying to achieve, and help them achieve it. Influence follows usefulness.

## Managing down is also product management

If you lead people, the customer relationship runs in both directions. Your team is buying something from you too: clarity, protection, growth, and fair recognition.

The best managers I had treated their team as customers. They removed friction, made priorities obvious, and translated noise from above into a signal people could act on. When I moved from leading a team back to being an IC, I carried that lesson with me. Whether or not you have direct reports, someone depends on your clarity.

## The unspoken

**Not all work is equal, even when the effort is.** Two projects can take the same number of hours. One sits on a slide your skip-level presents to the executive team. The other sits in a backlog nobody reads. Choosing your problem is the highest-leverage decision you make all year.

**Your manager cannot sell what they cannot explain.** If your impact cannot be summarised in one sentence by someone else, it will not survive the room where rewards are decided. Help them by writing that sentence for them.

**Customers churn quietly.** Nobody tells you your subscription is at risk. Stakeholders simply stop inviting you. Notice the silence.

## Low, good, exceptional

| | Low | Good | Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Customer focus | "This is what I like doing." | "This is what I was asked to do." | "This is the problem that matters most - here is my plan." |
| Managing up | Surprises their manager | Keeps their manager informed | Makes their manager more successful |
| Managing down | Hoards context | Shares context | Creates clarity others can act on |
| Problem choice | Comfortable | Assigned | Chosen for leverage |

## Try this week

1. **Name your three customers.** One line each: what they buy from you today.
2. **Run a discovery conversation.** Ask your manager: "What would make this half a success for you, and where can I take the most off your plate?"
3. **Write your one-sentence impact statement** - the one you want your manager to say about you in a room you are not in.

> Do not fall in love with your work. Fall in love with your customer's problem.
