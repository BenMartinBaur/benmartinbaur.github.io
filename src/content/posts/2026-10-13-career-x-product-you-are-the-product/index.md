Every year, somewhere in a big company, two people with the same title, the same manager, and roughly the same hours walk into the same review cycle. One leaves with a promotion. The other leaves with "great year, keep it up."

Neither of them is lazy. Neither of them is untalented. The difference is rarely effort. The difference is that one of them was running a product, and the other was doing a job.

This is the first episode of **Your Career x Your Product** - a series about the things I have observed in fifteen years of corporate life, from racking servers to leading a 36-person technical organisation across EMEA, to moving back into an individual contributor role in the Office of the CTO. It is about the parts of a career nobody writes down: how promotions really happen, why some people get rewarded and others get thanked, what separates good from exceptional, and how to manage up and down without losing yourself.

The thesis is simple.

> You are the product. Your organisation is the customer. Your career is the roadmap.

## Why a product lens

Products do not succeed because they exist. They succeed because they solve a painful problem for a customer who is willing to pay, and because enough people know about them, trust them, and recommend them.

Read that sentence again and replace "product" with "you".

- You need a **customer** - your business, your manager, your community.
- You need a **problem** worth solving - not the one you enjoy, the one that hurts.
- You need a **price** - your salary today, your promotion tomorrow, and the justification for both.
- You need **distribution** - visibility, brand, a story people can repeat.
- You need **advocates and sponsors** - people who put their name behind yours when you are not in the room.
- You need a **roadmap** - skills and capabilities you ship, iterate, and sometimes deprecate.

Most career advice addresses one of these at a time. Work hard. Network more. Get a mentor. Be visible. Each is true. None is sufficient. A product with great engineering and no go-to-market dies quietly. A product with great marketing and no substance dies loudly. Careers are no different.

## Why now

For most of corporate history, expertise was scarce. Knowing the answer was a moat.

That moat is drying up. Today, everyone has a PhD, a strategy consultant, and a VP in their pocket. An AI assistant will draft your architecture, critique your business case, and rewrite your executive summary before you finish your coffee. When answers become cheap, value moves somewhere else - to the questions, the judgement, the trust, and the ability to take something from idea to outcome end to end.

Elena Verna describes this shift in [IC work is the new career flex](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/ic-work-is-the-new-career-flex): the rise of the high-impact individual contributor who delivers business value end to end, without a team underneath. I have lived both sides of that equation. I built and led a cross-EMEA team, and then deliberately stepped back into an IC role. Not as a step down. As a repositioning of the product.

When the market shifts, great product teams do not complain about the market. They reposition. That is the mindset this series is about.

## The Career Product Canvas

Every episode in this series builds on one framework. I call it the **Career Product Canvas**. Fill it in honestly, once per quarter.

| Block | The product question | The career question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Customer | Who pays? | Who benefits from my work, and who decides my reward? |
| Problem | What pain do we solve? | Which problem on my manager's and my business's list hurts the most? |
| Value proposition | Why us, not the alternative? | What can I do that the next-best option - a peer, a vendor, an AI agent - cannot? |
| Pricing | What is it worth? | What evidence justifies my current level, and the next one? |
| Distribution | How do people find us? | How does my impact travel when I am not in the room? |
| Advocates | Who recommends us? | Who would sponsor me, and do they know what to say? |
| Roadmap | What ships next? | Which capability am I building, and which am I retiring? |

If you cannot answer a block in one sentence, that is your next piece of work.

## The unspoken

Here is the first uncomfortable truth of the series: **your company is not grading your effort. It is grading the value it can see.**

That is not cynicism. It is physics. Decision makers have limited attention, limited information, and a limited budget. They reward what they understand. If your impact needs a forty-minute explanation, it will lose to someone else's impact that fits in one sentence.

The second truth: **nobody owns your career roadmap but you.** Your manager owns a team plan. HR owns a process. Your sponsor owns their own reputation. You are the only product manager this product has.

## Three kinds of products

Over the years I have seen the same three patterns repeat, across functions, levels, and geographies.

| | Low performance | Good performance | Exceptional performance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Product mindset | "I do what I am asked." | "I deliver what I committed." | "I solve the problem behind the ask." |
| Customer | Themselves | Their manager | Their manager, the business, and the community |
| Value | Activity | Output | Outcome that changes the system |
| Visibility | Invisible or noisy | Known by the team | Known for something specific |
| Roadmap | None | Next promotion | Next problem worth owning |

Most people sit in the middle column. It is a respectable place to be. It is also crowded - and in an AI-assisted world, output alone gets cheaper every month. Episode three goes deep into what moves someone from good to exceptional. Spoiler: it is not more hours.

## The inner game

Michael Gervais often talks about the power of a clear personal philosophy - a short articulation of who you are and how you want to show up, especially under pressure. Products have the same thing. It is called a mission.

Before you optimise your price, your brand, or your sponsors, answer the question no framework can answer for you: **what do you want this product to stand for?**

Mine is written at the bottom of my About page: lead by example, inspire with passion, and treat every day as a chance to reset, learn, and unlock potential. It is not a slogan. It is a filter. When I am unsure whether to take on a project, I check it against that line.

Without a mission, a product drifts toward whatever the loudest customer wants. Careers drift the same way.

## Try this week

1. **Fill in the Career Product Canvas.** One sentence per block. Be honest where it is empty.
2. **Write your mission in one line.** Not your job title. What you want to be known for.
3. **Ask your manager one question:** "What is the most painful problem on your plate that nobody owns?" Do not answer it yet. Just listen.
