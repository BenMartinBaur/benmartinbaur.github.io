*Part 4 of a 4-part series on politics, visibility, performance and the new career flex.*

In the first three parts I looked at Ethan Evans' honest account of corporate politics at Amazon: reorgs, headcount as status, visibility, stack ranking and PIPs.

Almost all of it is about one currency: **people.** Who owns them, who gets more, who loses some.

So I found it striking to read [Elena Verna's essay "IC work is the new career flex"](https://www.elenaverna.com/p/ic-work-is-the-new-career-flex). Verna drives growth at Lovable as an IC, and has held senior growth roles at companies including SurveyMonkey, Miro, Amplitude and Dropbox. Her argument goes after that currency directly.

## The headcount trap, from the other side

Verna remembers a recruiter's first question on a call for a senior role: how many people she was managing. She had three, the role required more, and she was out. Not output, not impact, just headcount.

That is the same incentive Evans describes when a promotion level gets tied to org size. Two very different careers, the same lesson: **when headcount is the scoreboard, people optimise for headcount.**

Verna also points out that the system promotes people *out* of the work they're good at, and that when she reached senior manager level her own boss told her to stop writing SQL and tickets if she wanted to become a Director. (To be fair, she says she is grateful for that push at the time.)

## Enter the Hi-C

Her answer is the **High-Impact Individual Contributor (Hi-C)**: someone, often a former manager or executive, who can deliver a project with real business value end to end, without a team reporting to them.

She is living it herself. She moved back into an IC role at Lovable and describes building and shipping a pricing-page prototype on her own, work that used to need a PM, a designer and engineers.

What makes this possible now, in her view, is AI. She calls it "average intelligence" (a framing she credits to Ravi Mehta). It isn't brilliant, but it means one person can be at least average in disciplines outside their own craft. Her key line for me: it is becoming *"cheaper to just try something than to debate it."*

## What I see with customers

This matches a shift I see in my engagements at the Innovation Hub. The most effective people in AI projects right now are often senior people who have *gone back to building*: architects who prototype the agent themselves instead of writing a 40-page concept, or business leaders who test a workflow before asking for a team.

It also fits what Uli Homann and others keep saying in the [Armchair Architects](https://youtu.be/wTg9tFwjAOc) series: start with the outcome, then decide how much structure you actually need.

## Politics doesn't disappear. It moves.

Here is where I challenge the optimistic version.

Verna names two things you need to make Hi-Cs work: **open up access to information**, and **recruit leaders into IC roles**. Both are political problems. If headcount stops being the currency, people will compete for the next scarce thing:

- **Context:** who gets into the room where strategy happens.
- **Trust:** who leaders let run end to end without approval.
- **Attention:** whose prototype gets seen first.

That is exactly Evans' visibility playbook, just without the org chart. In a flatter, AI-native organisation, *good work doesn't speak for itself* becomes even more true, because nobody is reporting upward for you.

## Challenging the advice: what the coaches would say

- **Simon Sinek** would probably welcome the Hi-C as a step towards the *infinite game*: status comes from progress on a cause, not from winning headcount. He would also warn that removing layers without a **circle of safety** just moves the anxiety around.
- **Michael Gervais** would ask the question Verna asks herself: *did I level up, or give up status?* That is FOPO in its purest form. The Hi-C path needs people who can let go of how a title looks to others.
- **Toto Wolff** would point out that even the best driver needs a team. A Hi-C is not a solo act. It works because the system around them (data, platforms, colleagues, a no-blame culture) lets them move fast.

## My takeaways for the series

1. **Incentives beat principles.** Look at what you measure: headcount, visibility, or impact.
2. **Visibility is a duty, not vanity.** Report outcomes, name contributors, don't stretch the truth.
3. **High standards need high safety.** One without the other fails.
4. **AI changes the currency, not the game.** Context, trust and attention are the new headcount, so design for them on purpose.
