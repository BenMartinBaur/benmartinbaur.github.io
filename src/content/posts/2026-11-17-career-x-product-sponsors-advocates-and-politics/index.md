Say the word "politics" in a corporate setting and watch people flinch. It conjures backroom deals, credit stealing, and people who climb by stepping on others.

That exists. I have seen it. But it is not what politics is. Politics is simply **how decisions get made through people**. Every organisation larger than a handful of people has politics, because every organisation has more ideas than budget and more people than seats at the table.

Opting out of politics does not make you principled. It makes you absent from the decisions that shape your work.

In [episode five](?post=career-x-product-hire-your-own-product-marketing-manager) we built distribution. This episode is about channel partners: the people who carry your product into rooms you cannot enter, and how to navigate the system with integrity.

## What former executives say out loud

Ethan Evans, a former Amazon VP, has been unusually candid about this. In conversations such as [How Corporate Politics Work And How To Win](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=6WaeGfLnRvc) and the darker follow-up, [Asking Amazon VP Questions On Corporate Politics](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KhjhRRz3VJw), his themes are consistent: understand what the people around you want, build relationships before you need them, and recognise that decisions about you are made by people who mostly see you through other people's eyes.

That last point is the whole game. Most of your career is decided by people who have spent less than an hour with you, collectively. What fills the gap is what others say.

## Mentor, advocate, sponsor

These three words are used interchangeably. They are not the same.

| Role | What they do | What they risk | Product equivalent |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mentor | Gives advice and perspective | Time | Advisor |
| Advocate | Speaks well of you when asked | Little | Happy customer review |
| Sponsor | Uses their influence to create opportunities for you | Their reputation | Strategic partner who bets on you |

Mentors talk **to** you. Advocates talk **about** you. Sponsors talk **for** you - and put their own credibility on the line.

Most people have mentors. Fewer have advocates. Very few have real sponsors. That gap explains a great deal about who moves up.

## How sponsorship actually forms

You cannot ask someone to be your sponsor and expect it to work. Sponsorship is earned, and it usually follows a pattern:

1. **Proximity.** A senior leader sees your work first-hand - often on a problem they care about.
2. **Proof.** You deliver something that makes them look good or solves a problem that mattered to them.
3. **Trust.** You repeat this. They learn you are reliable under pressure.
4. **Bet.** They put your name forward for something - a project, a role, a nomination.
5. **Return.** You deliver on the bet. Their credibility grows. The relationship deepens.

The fastest path to a sponsor is to solve a problem a senior leader cares about, in a way they can see.

## Map your influence

Product teams map their stakeholders. Do the same.

Draw your influence map. Put yourself in the centre. Around you, place:

- **Decision makers** - people who decide your scope, rating, or promotion.
- **Influencers** - people whose opinion decision makers trust.
- **Partners** - peers whose success is coupled to yours.
- **Detractors** - people who are sceptical, for good or bad reasons.

For each, ask: what do they care about? What do they know about my work? What would they say about me today?

The answers are usually uncomfortable. Good. Now you know where the work is.

## Managing up, down, and across

Here is the framework I have used as both manager and individual contributor.

**Managing up**

- Know your manager's top three priorities and connect your work to them.
- No surprises. Bad news early, with a proposed path.
- Make them look good in rooms you are not in. Give them the sentence.
- Disagree in private, commit in public.

**Managing down**

- Create clarity: what matters, why, and what good looks like.
- Shield the team from noise; do not shield them from context.
- Sponsor your people. Say their names in rooms they are not in.
- Give credit publicly, feedback privately.

**Managing across**

- Build relationships before you need them.
- Help other teams win; reciprocity is the currency of large organisations.
- Resolve conflict at the lowest level possible, quickly and directly.

When I led a team of 36, the most important part of my job was not technical. It was being the sponsor for people the system could not yet see. Now, as an individual contributor, I try to do the same - because sponsorship is not a function of title. It is a function of credibility.

## The darker side

It would be dishonest to pretend everything is fair. Some people take credit for work they did not do. Some decisions are made in conversations you will never hear about. Some leaders reward loyalty over results.

Three principles have kept me grounded:

1. **Document your impact.** Credit theft is much harder when the record is clear.
2. **Diversify your sponsors.** A single sponsor is a single point of failure. Reorgs happen.
3. **Know your line.** Decide, in advance, what you will not do to win. Michael Gervais would call this living by your personal philosophy. Under pressure, you will not have time to decide. Decide now.

Toto Wolff has spoken about building a culture where people can raise problems without fear. You cannot always choose the culture you work in. You can choose to be that culture for the people around you.

## The unspoken

**Your reputation arrives before you do.** Decision makers often form a view of you from two or three secondhand data points. Manage those data points.

**Neutral is not safe.** If nobody in the room has a strong positive view of you, you will usually lose to someone who has one.

**Sponsorship is reciprocal.** Sponsors invest in people who make them successful. Ask what you are giving back.

## Low, good, exceptional

| | Low | Good | Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Politics | Avoids or games it | Understands it | Navigates it with integrity and helps others do the same |
| Relationships | Transactional | Friendly | Built on mutual value and trust |
| Sponsors | None | Mentors and advocates | Multiple sponsors across the organisation |
| Managing up | Surprises | Informs | Makes their manager successful |
| Managing down | Controls | Supports | Sponsors |

## Try this week

1. **Draw your influence map.** Decision makers, influencers, partners, detractors. Be honest.
2. **Identify one senior leader** whose priority you could help solve. Find a way to be useful, not visible.
3. **Sponsor someone.** Say their name, with a specific example of their impact, in a room they are not in.

> Politics is not the opposite of merit. It is how merit gets seen.
