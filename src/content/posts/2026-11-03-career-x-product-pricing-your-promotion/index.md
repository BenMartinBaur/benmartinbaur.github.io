No product team raises its price because it worked hard last quarter. It raises its price because customers get more value than they pay for, and the market accepts it.

A promotion is a price increase. A top reward is a premium tier. A pay rise is a renewal at a better rate. Once you see it this way, much of what feels unfair or mysterious about promotion cycles starts to make sense - not always fair, but understandable.

In [episode three](?post=career-x-product-low-good-exceptional) we defined product quality. This episode is about pricing: how to justify why your product, output, and impact are worth more.

## How promotions usually work

I will not describe any one company's process. But having been on both sides - as an individual contributor making a case and as a manager representing my team - I have seen patterns that hold across most large organisations.

1. **Budget comes first.** There is a finite pool for promotions and rewards. Your case does not compete against a standard. It competes against other cases.
2. **Someone else tells your story.** In most systems, you are not in the room. Your manager, and sometimes their manager, represents you.
3. **Evidence beats effort.** The question is not "did they work hard?" It is "are they already operating at the next level, and can we prove it?"
4. **Calibration is comparative.** Your work is placed next to peers'. Clarity and comparability win.
5. **Sponsors tip the balance.** When two cases are close, the one with a senior voice saying "I have seen this person's impact first-hand" usually wins.

None of this is a secret. It is just rarely said out loud.

## You are promoted for the level you already operate at

This is the most important sentence in the episode, and the one most people learn too late.

Promotions are usually a recognition, not a bet. The organisation is not asking "could this person do the next job?" It is asking "has this person been doing the next job for a while, consistently?"

That means the work for your next promotion started months ago. If you start building the case when the cycle opens, you are already late.

## The pricing evidence

Products justify their price with proof: case studies, metrics, testimonials, benchmarks. Your career needs the same.

| Evidence type | Product equivalent | Career example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Outcomes | Metrics and ROI | Revenue influenced, cost reduced, risk removed, time saved |
| Scope | Market size | Number of teams, customers, or regions affected |
| Complexity | Technical moat | Ambiguity resolved, conflicting stakeholders aligned |
| Leverage | Network effects | Others enabled, assets reused, practices adopted |
| Testimonials | Customer quotes | Feedback from stakeholders, peers, and leaders |
| Consistency | Track record | Sustained over multiple cycles, not one heroic quarter |

Keep a running impact log. Every week, one line: what you did, what changed, who benefited, and the proof. When the cycle opens, you are curating, not inventing.

## Write the brief for your manager

Your manager is your sales team in a room you cannot enter. Make their job easy.

Give them a one-page brief:

- **The headline.** One sentence: the most important impact you had.
- **Three proof points.** Each with an outcome, a scope, and a number or a quote.
- **The next-level signal.** Where you already operated at the level above, with an example.
- **The ask.** What you are hoping for, stated plainly.

When I managed a team, the people who did this got better representation. Not because I favoured them, but because I could defend their case with precision while others' cases relied on my memory.

## Awards are a different market

Awards are not promotions. Promotions price your level. Awards recognise a moment of exceptional impact - often impact that went far beyond the role.

Receiving the Pinnacle Award - one of 43 recipients out of more than 228,000 employees - taught me something important: the work that gets recognised at that level is almost never the work you were asked to do. It is the work you chose to do because it mattered - building communities, enabling others at scale, and delivering customer outcomes that changed how a team operated. The award followed the impact. It was never the target.

If you chase awards, you will optimise for visibility. If you chase impact that changes the system, the recognition tends to find you - provided someone tells the story. That is next episode.

## The unspoken

**Your price is anchored by your last negotiation.** Entry level, starting salary, and early ratings shape your trajectory more than most people realise. Changing teams or companies can reset the anchor - for better or worse.

**"Not this time" is information, not a verdict.** Ask exactly what evidence was missing. Then ship that evidence. Do not argue the past cycle; prepare the next one.

**Promotion is not the only price signal.** Scope, ownership of a strategic problem, access to senior forums, and the ability to choose your work are all forms of compensation. Sometimes they are worth more than a title.

**Some people move up because they manoeuvre, not because they deliver.** It happens. Do not copy it. Products built on marketing alone eventually get found out - and so do careers.

## Low, good, exceptional

| | Low | Good | Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Promotion mindset | "I deserve it." | "I hit my goals." | "I have been operating at the next level for months - here is the proof." |
| Evidence | Memory | Goal tracker | Impact log with outcomes, scope, and testimonials |
| Timing | Starts at cycle | Starts a quarter before | Always on |
| After a "no" | Resentment | Disappointment | Diagnosis, plan, and execution |

## Try this week

1. **Start an impact log.** One line per week. Outcome, beneficiary, proof.
2. **Read the next-level expectations** for your role and mark where you already have evidence and where you have gaps.
3. **Draft your one-page brief** - even if no cycle is near. It will show you what is missing.

> You do not get paid for potential. You get paid for proof.
