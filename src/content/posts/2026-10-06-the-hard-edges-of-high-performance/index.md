*Part 3 of a 4-part series on politics, visibility, performance and the new career flex.*

This is the darkest part of the series, and the most important to read carefully.

In one of his conversations with Ryan Peterman, retired Amazon VP **Ethan Evans** says something that most managers would only say in private: *"I think most PIPs are a combination of dishonest and or psychologically unrealistic."*

I'm not writing this to bash any company. Performance management is hard everywhere, including in the organisations I work with. But this is an unusually candid look inside one of the most studied performance cultures, so it is worth looking at clearly.

## Fired twice, then a VP

Evans' own story starts with failure. Before Amazon, he was let go twice at startups. He describes himself back then as combative and abrasive. After the second time, he says, he asked himself who the common element was: *"it's me."*

He then spent over 15 years at Amazon, became a Vice President, and says he had about 50 meetings each with Jeff Bezos and Andy Jassy (his own estimate).

This is the part of the series I find most hopeful. He also credits learned soft skills, alliances and results, but the turning point I take from his story is **honest self-diagnosis**.

**Label:** Evans' account. The quote is verified in the transcript. The meeting count is his estimate.

## Unregretted attrition: the system behind the stories

Evans describes Amazon's **unregretted attrition (URA)** goal as a yearly target for people the company wants to leave. He says it was usually about 7%, sometimes as low as 4%. Managers could improve someone's performance, have them quit, or fire them, but they had to hit the number.

Independent reporting supports this in general terms. Business Insider [reported in 2021 (syndicated)](https://tech.yahoo.com/business/articles/amazon-managers-hire-people-intend-131144081.html) on URA targets and on allegations that some managers hired people specifically to fire them to meet those goals. *The New York Times* covered Amazon's performance culture in depth in its 2015 article ["Inside Amazon"](https://www.nytimes.com/2015/08/16/technology/inside-amazon-wrestling-big-ideas-in-a-bruising-workplace.html). Amazon has publicly disputed parts of that reporting, and exact percentages vary by source and year.

**Label:** Evans' account plus independent reporting. Not an official Amazon disclosure.

## The experiment that surprised me

Here is the nuance most commentators miss. Evans says Amazon once **removed** the URA goal after years of bad press. What happened next, according to him: managers *stopped having hard conversations* with struggling performers. Without pressure, it was easier to focus on the good people and let the problem stay, especially when the person was well liked.

That is uncomfortable, because it is true in many places I visit. Organisations without forced ranking often don't have kinder performance cultures. They have *avoidant* ones. People find out they are underperforming in a restructuring instead of in a conversation.

So the real question is not "ranking or no ranking". It is: **how do you make honest conversations normal without forcing a quota of losers?**

## PIPs and the "50 ways"

Evans also says a manager in good standing can remove almost any one employee, and that there are "like 50 ways to do it", starting with worse assignments and fewer promotion conversations. He describes PIPs as often being a formality after the decision is already made.

If you're an employee, his practical advice is to see the signals early and act on your own terms. If you're a leader, I think the lesson is harder: **if your PIPs are theatre, your people know it, and everything else you say about growth loses credibility.**

## Challenging the advice

**Toto Wolff** has built one of the most successful teams in Formula 1 around a different principle. He has described Mercedes' approach as blaming *the problem, not the person*, and taking responsibility himself when things go wrong ([GPblog](https://www.gpblog.com/en/news/toto-wolff-on-his-leadership-at-mercedes.html)). His reasoning is performance, not kindness: if people fear blame, they hide mistakes, and in F1 hidden mistakes cost races.

**Amy Edmondson** of Harvard Business School, whose research defined **psychological safety** in teams ([HBS faculty profile](https://www.hbs.edu/faculty/Pages/profile.aspx?facId=6451)), would add that high standards and safety are not opposites. Her model puts the high-performing quadrant where *both* are high. Low safety with high standards creates anxiety. High safety with low standards creates comfort.

Evans' URA experiment shows what can happen when a hard target is removed without building strong feedback and accountability habits in its place. Many forced-ranking stories show the opposite failure: anxiety. Wolff and Edmondson point to the hard middle: **very high standards, honest feedback, and no fear of telling the truth.**

## My takeaways

1. The most useful career skill in this story is honest self-diagnosis.
2. Removing hard targets without building feedback habits leads to avoidance, not kindness.
3. Aim for high standards *and* high safety. One without the other fails.
