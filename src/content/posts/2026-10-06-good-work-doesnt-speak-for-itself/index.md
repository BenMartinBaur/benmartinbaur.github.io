*Part 2 of a 4-part series on politics, visibility, performance and the new career flex.*

In nearly every engineering team I work with, there is one person who built the hard part, and someone else who presented it to leadership.

Usually that is nobody's bad intention. It is just how reporting lines work. But it shapes careers.

In his conversations with Ryan Peterman, retired Amazon VP **Ethan Evans** is very direct about this. His message is: *staying silent and hoping your part gets noticed is the one approach that never works.*

## Opting out is also a choice

Evans has little patience for people who refuse to "play politics" on principle. In his words, they tell themselves they are a *"bold martyr for honesty and all you are is a martyr."*

That is harsh. But I recognise the pattern. I've seen brilliant architects watch their ideas get presented by others, and then decide that self-promotion is beneath them. A year later they are frustrated and their ideas have less reach.

Evans' alternative is not to become loud. It is to make what you say *worth hearing*.

## Evans' visibility playbook

From the episodes, his practical advice comes down to a few moves:

- **Speak early.** The person who first shares "we shipped this" usually shapes the story. Use "we" and name the collaborators.
- **Add technical colour.** If a product manager reports upward, ask to be included. If that doesn't happen, follow up with a short technical note for leaders who want to know how it works.
- **Use leadership review forums.** He noticed that some people asked for steering or headcount in leadership reviews, when the real goal was to get the project seen before promotion time.
- **Be "strategically annoying".** He likes this phrase because annoying is not abrasive. It means having an opinion and being willing to fight for it.

## Where it gets uncomfortable

Evans also says you should claim everything you legitimately can, and *"stretch legitimate a little bit against the limits."* His own example is saying he was the first engineer on Prime Video. He admits one person challenged him on it, because the product he worked on was Amazon's first video product, before it became Prime Video.

I appreciate the honesty. I also think this is where the line sits. Claiming your real contribution is a professional duty. Stretching it makes it harder for people to trust you, and in my experience trust is the scarcest resource in any leadership team.

## Challenging the advice

**Michael Gervais**, a high-performance psychologist and host of *Finding Mastery*, writes about **FOPO, the fear of other people's opinions**, in *The First Rule of Mastery* ([findingmastery.com](https://www.findingmastery.com/)). His view is that when we optimise for how others see us, we lose access to our best performance.

That is a real tension with a visibility playbook. If visibility becomes the goal, you end up managing perception instead of doing great work. If it is a *tool*, so that the right people know enough to make good decisions, then it supports mastery.

**Kim Scott**'s *Radical Candor* adds a second test: care personally *and* challenge directly ([radicalcandor.com](https://www.radicalcandor.com/)). Applied to credit, it's simple. Say clearly what you did, and say just as clearly what others did. Credit that names other people is candour. Credit that quietly leaves them out drifts towards what Scott calls "manipulative insincerity", where you neither care nor challenge.

My own rule in customer programmes: **report outcomes, name contributors, and let the evidence do the stretching.**

## My takeaways

1. Silence is not neutral. It hands the story to someone else.
2. Visibility is a tool for good decisions, not a performance.
3. Claim what is yours, name what is theirs, and don't stretch the truth.
