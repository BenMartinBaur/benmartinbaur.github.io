Every great product has a version history. Version 1.0 proves the idea. Version 2.0 fixes what the market taught you. Version 3.0 often looks nothing like the original.

Careers are the same, except most people never ship a new version. They keep patching 1.0 - adding responsibilities, accepting more meetings, layering skills on top of skills - until the product is slow, bloated, and hard to explain.

This is the final episode of the first season of **Your Career x Your Product**. We have defined the product, found the customer, raised the quality, set the price, built the brand, earned sponsors, and repositioned for the AI era. Now the question is: how do you keep iterating, year after year, without losing yourself?

## Run the retro

Toto Wolff has described a culture where every race - win or lose - is followed by a rigorous, blame-free debrief. Not to assign fault. To find the next tenth of a second.

Most careers never get a debrief. We move from cycle to cycle, celebrating the wins and quietly avoiding the losses. Then we wonder why the same patterns keep repeating.

Run a career retro at least once a year. Here is the format I use:

| Question | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| What did I ship that mattered? | Separates outcomes from activity |
| What did customers - business, manager, community - value most? | Tests product-market fit |
| Where did I underperform, and why? | Honest diagnosis, no blame |
| What drained me? What energised me? | Sustainability is a performance input |
| What did I learn that changes how I work? | Captures compounding knowledge |
| What will I stop, start, and continue? | Turns reflection into a roadmap |

Write it down. Share parts of it with your manager or a trusted peer. The point is not the document. The point is the decision that comes out of it.

## Deprecate features

Product teams know that every feature has a maintenance cost. Old features slow down new ones. Great teams deprecate deliberately.

Your career has features too: responsibilities you took on years ago, skills you are known for but no longer want to use, meetings you attend out of habit, roles you play because nobody else will.

Ask:

- Which of my current responsibilities would I not take on if offered today?
- Which skill am I known for that no longer fits my roadmap?
- Who could own this better than I can - and grow by doing it?

Deprecating is not quitting. It is making room. When I stepped back from leading a large team into an individual contributor role, I had to deprecate some of the work that had defined me. It was uncomfortable. It also created space for the work I do now.

> What you stop doing defines your next version as much as what you start.

## Ship new features

Then decide what to build. A good career feature sits at the intersection of three things:

1. **Customer demand.** A capability your business, manager, or community will need in the next two years.
2. **Personal energy.** Something you are genuinely curious about, so you will practise it when nobody is checking.
3. **Real practice.** An actual opportunity to use it on a real problem, with feedback.

Pick one or two. Ship something visible with each. Use the feedback to decide whether to go deeper or pivot. This is the same principle I wrote about in [build a career portfolio, not a ladder](?post=build-a-career-portfolio): treat capabilities like compounding assets and choose your learning bets deliberately.

## Know when to pivot

Sometimes iteration is not enough. Sometimes the product needs a pivot: a new team, a new role, a new company, a new craft.

Signals worth paying attention to:

- You have stopped learning for more than a year.
- Your customers no longer value what you are best at.
- Your mission and your daily work have drifted apart.
- You are winning, but you no longer care about the game.

A pivot is not a failure of the original product. It is evidence that you are listening to the market - and to yourself.

## Protect the inner game

Michael Gervais has spent his career working with elite athletes and leaders on the part of performance nobody sees: the mind. Presence. Self-awareness. Calm under pressure. A clear personal philosophy to return to when the environment gets loud.

High-performing careers are long games. The people I have seen sustain exceptional impact over decades share a few habits:

- **They separate identity from outcome.** A missed promotion is an event, not a verdict on who they are.
- **They train recovery.** Energy is managed like a budget, not spent like an unlimited resource.
- **They have a reset ritual.** A walk, a board in the water, a set behind the decks - something that brings them back to themselves. Mine involves surfing and music.
- **They return to their why.** Not the title. The reason they started.

> I lead by example, inspire with passion, and treat every day as a chance to reset, learn, and unlock potential.

That line is not decoration on my About page. It is the operating system that makes every other episode in this series sustainable.

## Ambition with humility

There is a balance I admire in the best leaders I have watched, in sport and in business. They state their ambition plainly - they want to win, and they say so. And they carry their wins lightly, credit their team, and go straight back to work.

That is the posture I hope this series encourages. Be ambitious about your career. Treat it with the seriousness of a product you are proud of. And stay humble enough to keep learning from every customer, every setback, and every debrief.

## The unspoken

**Nobody will tell you it is time for version 2.0.** The system is happy for you to stay in version 1.0 forever, as long as it works for the system.

**Your past success is your biggest constraint.** The things that got you here are the hardest to let go of, because they worked.

**Rest is not a reward for performance. It is a requirement for it.**

## Low, good, exceptional

| | Low | Good | Exceptional |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Reflection | None | At review time | Regular, honest, blame-free retros |
| Iteration | Patches the old version | Adds new features | Deprecates deliberately and ships new versions |
| Pivots | Stuck or reactive | Considered occasionally | Made on purpose, driven by signals |
| Inner game | Identity tied to outcomes | Some resilience | Clear philosophy, sustainable energy, steady under pressure |

## Try this week

1. **Run your career retro** using the six questions above.
2. **Deprecate one feature.** Hand over, stop, or say no to one responsibility that no longer fits.
3. **Define your version 2.0** in three sentences: what it does, for whom, and what makes it different from version 1.0.

> Ship it. Learn from it. Make the next version better. That is the whole game.
